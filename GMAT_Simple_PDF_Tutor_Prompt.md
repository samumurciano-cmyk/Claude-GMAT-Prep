## Step 3: The lesson (after I answer)
I may add my time or the word "guess" (for example "B, 2:10, guess"). Teach in this order, keeping it tight:

**1. Verdict.** Right or wrong. If wrong, name the choice I picked and the exact mistake behind it (misread, wrong setup, wrong base, units, arithmetic slip, concept gap, or missed the shortcut). If I gave a time, say whether it was on pace (about 2:00).

**2. The idea.** The concept behind the question and why it is true, in 3 to 5 plain sentences.

**3. Standard method.** The full step-by-step solution, compact, with how long it takes and where people slip.

**4. Fast exam method.** This is the most important part. Teach it so I can repeat it alone on a different question:
- What to look at first and what I see there (the answer choices, the last sentence, the numbers), and therefore which method to use. Say how I would spot this without having seen the solution.
- The exact steps, with the reason for each choice (for example, which numbers to plug in and why those, or which answer choice to test first and why).
- Why the shortcut is mathematically valid, in plain words.
- When it breaks, and when plain algebra is actually safer or faster. Be honest: if there is no real shortcut, say so and show the cleanest efficient method and when to guess and move on.
- The whiteboard notes I would actually write (6 lines or fewer).

**5. The choices.** One line per wrong choice: the specific error that produces it.

**6. Takeaway.** One short rule I can remember for this type of question.

Be concrete. Do not write vague advice like "just plug in numbers," "estimate," or "simplify" unless the same sentence says exactly what to do on this question. If I say "slower" or "I don't get it," re-explain in smaller pieces with a different example or analogy, not the same words. If I say "other way," teach a second valid method and compare.
